Where could a delivery drone actually fly in Manhattan?

I mapped when and where food orders are likely to land, then laid the FAA's low-altitude ceilings over the same streets to find the first routes that could work, and the ones that can't.

Hajong (HJ) Lim, process engineer in battery manufacturing. Data from January 2025 to July 2026.

The densest delivery market in the country is also its hardest sky.

Manhattan has the orders: short trips, dense towers, and streets where a courier spends as much time parking and climbing stairs as riding. It also sits under the Class B airspace of JFK, LaGuardia and Newark, needs an NYPD permit for every takeoff and landing, and still waits on a federal rule for flights beyond the pilot's line of sight.

So the useful question isn't whether drones will deliver here. It's which blocks, at which hours, could justify the first corridor.

When the orders come

There is no public order-level delivery data for New York, so demand here is modeled from Uber and Lyft trips that start or end in Manhattan, scaled to the city's reported weekly delivery volume.

The ceiling over every block

The FAA's UAS Facility Maps divide controlled airspace into a grid and set the highest altitude a small drone can be approved for in each cell. A zero means no automatic approval at all.

Each high-demand pair of neighborhoods gets a straight line and a detour that avoids low ceilings. The map shows which routes stay above the limit and which don't.

How demand moved in a year

Comparing the same months, January to July, total demand barely changed: about 155,400 estimated deliveries a day in 2025 against 154,100 in 2026. Underneath, it shifted. Most of the biggest gains were in upper Manhattan, with Financial District South as the exception, while the west side of Midtown and the blocks around the World Trade Center lost ground.

I haven't yet traced the causes of individual shifts, so this shows where demand moved, not why.

What I take from this

Uptown is where demand and airspace line up

The 13 busiest neighborhood pairs are all short hops of about 1 to 2 km in Harlem, East Harlem, Washington Heights and the Upper East Side. Every one of them fits under ceilings of 300 to 400 ft.

Downtown's busy pairs sit under the lowest ceilings

Routes between the Lower East Side, East Village, TriBeCa, West Village and Chelsea cross 0 ft cells. Six of the top 25 pairs stay blocked even with a detour.

Demand doesn't follow housing

The link between delivery demand and housing density is close to zero in every time window: −0.15 in the morning, −0.07 at lunch and +0.10 at dinner. Midtown Center leads the morning and lunch; the East Village leads dinner.

Where I'd start

Central Harlem North to East Harlem North. It's one of the busiest pairs at about 390 trips a day, 1.7 km apart, never below a 300 ft ceiling, and about 5 minutes faster than a car.

What this can't tell you

Orders are modeled. Delivery counts come from ride-hail trips scaled to citywide totals, not from real orders.

Routes are zone to zone. They connect neighborhood centers on a 100 m grid and ignore building heights.

Airspace is partial. Helicopter corridors, temporary flight restrictions and landing sites aren't modeled.

This isn't flight approval. It's a planning study. Real operations need FAA authorization and an NYPD permit.
